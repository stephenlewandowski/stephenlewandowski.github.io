# Exercise: From a Surveillance Signal to a Spatial Decision

**Estimated time:** 25–35 minutes  
**Format:** Teams of 3–4, followed by a short briefing

## Scenario

A fictional regional health office receives reports of a respiratory event from clinics serving six fictional areas during one four-week period. The office must decide what to verify first, where to consider additional investigation or outreach, and what level of geographic information can be shared publicly.

**Important:** Every place name and value below is synthetic instructional data. These are not official data, do not describe a real population, and must not be cited as evidence about any community. The classroom rule suppresses reported counts below 5; it is not presented as a universal agency threshold.

## Synthetic dataset

| Service area | Population | Reported events | Records complete | Reports received late | Context note |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Northgate | 52,000 | 78 | 94% | 7% | Stable clinic participation |
| Riverbend | 18,500 | 42 | 82% | 24% | Reporting workflow changed mid-period |
| Central | 81,000 | 96 | 97% | 4% | Regional referral center |
| Lakeside | 9,200 | 6 | 76% | 18% | Two small clinics |
| Hillview | 31,500 | 60 | 90% | 9% | Boundary revised last year |
| Southpoint | 4,800 | Suppressed (<5) | 88% | 11% | Public display restricted |

## Tasks

1. State the immediate public-health decision in one sentence. Is the described use surveillance/program practice, research, or unresolved? Explain using purpose rather than the data source alone.
2. Calculate the reported-event rate per 10,000 for each unsuppressed area. Do not infer or back-calculate Southpoint’s value.
3. Identify the area with the largest count and the area with the highest displayed rate. Explain why those findings could support different actions.
4. Select four surveillance or data-quality attributes most important to this decision. For each, name the evidence in the table and one piece of missing information.
5. Propose two choropleth designs: one that would likely mislead and one that would better support the decision. Specify value (count or rate), geographic unit, classification approach, and legend/caveat.
6. Explain how the following could change interpretation: Hillview’s boundary revision, Riverbend’s reporting change, Lakeside’s small numerator, and Southpoint’s suppression.
7. Write one ecological claim that the map cannot support. Then write a narrower area-level statement it can support.
8. Recommend one immediate action, one verification step, and one additional data element. Match the strength of each recommendation to the evidence.
9. Draft a three-sentence public communication using: “The data show…,” “The data do not establish…,” and “We recommend next…”. Include a privacy or suppression statement.

## Deliverable

Submit a one-page decision brief or give a three-minute briefing. Include the rate calculations, a decision-focused evidence table, the proposed map specification, and the three-sentence communication. Credit the dataset as “Surveillance and GIS synthetic instructional data, 2026.”

## Self-check before submission

- Did the numerator, denominator, geography, and period align?
- Did you treat suppression as unavailable rather than zero?
- Did you separate pattern, explanation, causation, and action?
- Did you state the most important data-quality and privacy limitations?
- Could a decision-maker tell what should happen next?
