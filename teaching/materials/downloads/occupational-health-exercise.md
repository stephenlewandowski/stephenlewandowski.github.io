# Occupational Health and Industrial Hygiene: Workplace Evidence-to-Action Exercise

## Currentness Notice

Evidence checked on 2026-08-09. OSHA's federal heat-specific standard remains proposed; existing enforcement authorities and the 2026 National Emphasis Program are separate from that proposal.

## Purpose

This exercise asks learners to build a defensible occupational-health response from a fictional workplace scenario.

## Scenario

A regional employer operates an indoor fabrication area and an outdoor maintenance crew. Employees may encounter airborne contaminants and seasonal heat. Management asks for one integrated plan that distinguishes enforceable requirements, recommended practices, sampling evidence, and pending rulemaking.

## Task 1 — Authority Map

Create a four-column map: claim, issuing organization, authority or effective status, and operational implication. Include at least one OSHA requirement, one NIOSH recommendation, and the proposed federal heat rule.

## Task 2 — Exposure Assessment Plan

Describe how representative and worst-case personal sampling, media selection, calibration, flow rate, duration, and laboratory-method coordination would fit into a staged assessment. Identify information that must be contaminant-specific.

## Task 3 — Control Strategy

Prioritize elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment. Explain why the hierarchy is a recommendation framework rather than a universal guarantee that one control is feasible.

## Task 4 — Heat Program

Draft a concise program covering acclimatization, hydration, rest and recovery, workload, monitoring, training, and emergency response. Keep the proposed heat standard separate from current enforcement authorities and the 2026 National Emphasis Program.

## Deliverable

Submit a two-page decision memo and a one-page evidence map. Every operational claim must identify whether it is a current requirement, current recommendation, enforcement context, or proposal.

## Assessment Criteria

- Correct source-authority classification.
- Transparent assumptions and uncertainty.
- Appropriate selection and ordering of controls.
- Clear separation of current enforcement from proposed rulemaking.
- Human-readable attribution to authoritative sources.

## Evidence Resources

- NIOSH orders the hierarchy of controls as elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment. — [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, *Hierarchy of Controls*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/hierarchy-of-controls/index.html)
- OSHA's personal air-sampling guidance covers representative and worst-case sampling, media selection, calibration, flow rate, duration, and analytical-method coordination. — [Occupational Safety and Health Administration, *OSHA Technical Manual Section II Chapter 1: Personal Sampling for Air Contaminants*](https://www.osha.gov/otm/section-2-health-hazards/chapter-1)
- OSHA's 29 CFR 1910.1000 Z tables contain federal permissible exposure limits; these must not be conflated with NIOSH recommended limits. — [Occupational Safety and Health Administration, *29 CFR 1910.1000 Air Contaminants and Z tables*](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.1000)
- OSHA's annotated PEL tables include non-OSHA recommendations for comparison; those values are not OSHA-enforceable limits. — [Occupational Safety and Health Administration, *OSHA Annotated Permissible Exposure Limits*](https://www.osha.gov/annotated-pels)
- The NIOSH Pocket Guide distinguishes NIOSH RELs from OSHA PELs and is a concise hazard guide rather than a substitute for standards or safety data sheets. — [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, *NIOSH Pocket Guide to Chemical Hazards*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/npg/default.html)
- The online NMAM fifth-edition collection is the current method-identification source; a consolidated older PDF may omit newer individual methods. — [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, *NIOSH Manual of Analytical Methods, fifth edition online collection*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/nmam/)
- OSHA's federal heat standard remains proposed; the rulemaking page does not establish a current final heat-specific standard. — [Occupational Safety and Health Administration, *Heat Injury and Illness Prevention rulemaking*](https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure/rulemaking)
- OSHA currently uses existing standards and enforcement authorities for heat hazards while the heat-specific rule remains proposed. — [Occupational Safety and Health Administration, *Heat exposure: current standards and enforcement*](https://www.osha.gov/heat-exposure)
- OSHA Directive CPL 03-00-024, dated April 10, 2026, establishes the current National Emphasis Program for outdoor and indoor heat-related hazards. — [Occupational Safety and Health Administration, *National Emphasis Program: Outdoor and Indoor Heat-Related Hazards*](https://www.osha.gov/enforcement/directives/cpl-03-00-024-0)
- NIOSH recommends heat-stress programs addressing acclimatization, hydration, rest/recovery, workload, monitoring, training, and emergency response. — [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, *Workplace Recommendations for Heat Stress*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/heat-stress/recommendations/)
- NIOSH's current acclimatization schedule for a new worker starts at 20% of usual heat-work duration on day 1 and increases by no more than 20 percentage points daily. — [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, *Acclimatization and PPE heat-burden guidance*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/heat-stress/recommendations/acclimatization.html)
- For a worker with recent heat experience, NIOSH recommends 50%, 60%, 80%, then 100% of usual heat-work duration over four days. — [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, *Acclimatization and PPE heat-burden guidance*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/heat-stress/recommendations/acclimatization.html)
- NIOSH Publication 2016-106 provides recommended occupational heat criteria and prevention practices; it is not an OSHA standard. — [National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, *Criteria for a Recommended Standard: Occupational Exposure to Heat and Hot Environments*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2016-106/default.html)
